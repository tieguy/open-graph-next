The article, enriched

The open knowledge ecosystem is already connected. Almost nothing shows you the connections.

These pages take an English Wikipedia article and render it with the open ecosystem's media and cited sources placed beside the prose — Internet Archive scans, Commons files, OpenLibrary editions, court opinions. Nothing is sourced or placed by hand. Every item was found by an identifier the article itself states: a citation's ISBN, OCLC or LCCN, or a wikilink's Wikidata QID. There is no per-article code and, on the three pages below, no curated dataset.

Technical approach

How the pages are built

Each page starts from the article's own wikitext. Two kinds of anchor come out of it: the identifiers in its citations — ISBN, OCLC, LCCN — and the Wikidata QIDs behind its wikilinks. Each anchor is looked up in the open ecosystem, and whatever comes back is placed in the section whose anchor found it. That rule is the whole design, and it is why there is no per-article code.

Each page is one self-contained HTML file, generated ahead of time — Apollo 11 is 905 KB and takes a few minutes to build, most of it spent waiting politely on other people's servers. That patience is not incidental. Nominally-open APIs are getting harder to use as institutions raise defences against scrapers and AI crawlers: rate limits tighten, blocks land without notice, and a well-behaved client now has to identify itself, throttle itself, and yield to human traffic to stay welcome. A cooperative ecosystem that is open in licence but closing in practice is its own kind of problem.

Where it gets hard

Four hard problems

Laying out data you have never seen

This one comes before any of the linking, and it is easy to underestimate. An arbitrary article gives you arbitrary shape: one section carries forty images and the next carries none, a cited book has a cover or it doesn't, and the article's own images arrive at whatever dimensions someone uploaded. A layout that only looks good on the article you designed it against is a demo, not a pipeline.

Two decisions do most of the work here. The page lets CSS reflow the prose around the media rather than computing positions, so a sparse section simply closes up instead of leaving a hole where a fixed column would have been. And every image is sized from its real dimensions, read out of the JPEG or PNG header when the API won't say, so nothing is ever squashed into a guessed aspect ratio.

The citations that hide the books

Wikipedia has two citation styles, and the better-sourced the article, the more likely it uses the harder one. Apollo 11 keeps 57 of its 65 citation anchors as {{sfn}} pointers into a pooled bibliography rather than in the footnotes themselves — so anything that reads footnotes alone misses most of the books on exactly the pages that cite the most books. They have to be joined back on surname and year.

The works with no identifier anywhere

Prandtl's 1899 dissertation is scanned and public at the Internet Archive. It carries no ISBN, no OCLC, no LCCN. Until this week Wikidata knew it as Q72419729 — an item with an author, a date and an institution, a label in Catalan and in no other language, and no pointer to the scan. Nothing was shared between the two records to join on, which is the normal condition for a newly-scanned collection rather than an exotic one.

So the match was made on the object Wikidata describes (P1026), and the page showed its work:

All three signals are required, because the same collection holds theses by Ludwig, Hans and Antonius Prandtl. The card is dashed and says corroborated, not identified, because a description that agrees is a weaker claim than an identifier that matches, and it must not be allowed to read like one.

But the more interesting move was upstream, and on 2 August 2026 we made it. Nothing about the gap was permanent: what made the thesis hard to find was a handful of facts nobody had written down, and writing them down is an edit anyone can make. Trying to display the collection is what surfaced it — you don't notice a missing identifier until something tries to follow it. Read the graph to find what's missing, then go fix it.

So Q72419729 now carries English and German labels, and — the one that matters — P724, the Internet Archive identifier, pointing straight at the scan. The title came off the scan's own title page: the Internet Archive's Kipp-Erscheinungen is the short form, dropping the subtitle ein Fall von instabilem elastischem Gleichgewicht.

The Prandtl page above no longer shows a dashed card. The pivot checks for a stated identifier before it corroborates anything, so the thesis is now found the ordinary way — one request instead of nine — and the corroborated legend has disappeared from that page because nothing on it is corroborated any more. Every other tool that joins these two collections gets the same benefit, without knowing this page exists.

The expensive half of this work is already done. Turning 319,341 dissertations into public scans took money and years, and it is the part that cannot be automated. What remains is the cheap half, and it is mostly not done: connecting each scan to the records that already describe its author, its subject and its institution. We connected one. Each connection is a single claim added to a record that already exists.

Which is what this page is really a proof of concept for. Not one thesis — the state of the collection once that linking work is done, where a scan appears next to the paragraph it belongs to, in whatever article, in whatever language, without anybody curating a page for it. The corroboration code stays because that state doesn't arrive all at once: for as long as most of the collection is unlinked, something has to be able to find a scan by what it is rather than by what it is called.

The ecosystem's own coverage

This is the real ceiling, and it is not a software problem. Of the unique identifiers cited across these three articles, most reach no Internet Archive record at all — the query is right and the answer is genuinely nothing. So each section states how many of its sources you can actually open, and a section of dead ends says so rather than just looking thin.

Nothing fills those gaps with a fuzzy title search. A guess sitting beside a verified match would poison every match on the page. And you can't yet type your own article in: these pages are pre-generated, and going live needs streaming and a server.
