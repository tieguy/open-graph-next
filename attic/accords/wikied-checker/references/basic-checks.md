title: Basic checks
---

These checks correspond to the sourcing skills that Wiki Education's student training emphasizes. They are the foundation of good Wikipedia editing. Every edit should pass these checks.

## Says who? — Verifiability (WP:V)

The threshold for inclusion in Wikipedia is *verifiability*, not truth. A claim belongs in Wikipedia because a reader can check it against a published, reliable source — not because an editor knows it to be true.

### What the checker looks for

- **Unsourced claims.** Any factual claim without an inline citation. As a baseline, every paragraph a student contributes should have at least one citation.
- **Material that must always be cited.** Four categories require inline citations regardless of context: (1) direct quotations, (2) material whose verifiability has been challenged, (3) material likely to be challenged, and (4) contentious material about living persons.
- **The burden of evidence.** The editor adding material is responsible for providing citations. Other editors are not obligated to find sources on your behalf. If you cannot find a reliable source for a claim, leave it out.

### Why it matters

Verifiability is what makes Wikipedia trustworthy. "Behind every great Wikipedia article is a collection of great sources." Without citations, readers have no way to evaluate whether the information is accurate, and other editors will likely remove or tag unsourced material.

See: [Wikipedia:Verifiability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability)

## Just the facts — Neutral point of view (WP:NPOV)

Wikipedia articles must represent all significant viewpoints fairly, proportionately, and without editorial bias. Editors must not use Wikipedia to advocate, persuade, or promote a position.

### What the checker looks for

- **Advocacy or persuasive language.** Text that argues for a position rather than describing what sources say. Words like "clearly," "obviously," "unfortunately," or "importantly" often signal editorial opinion.
- **Imbalanced presentation.** Covering one side of a controversy in detail while dismissing or omitting the other. Wikipedia requires presenting each significant view accurately, with context, giving each its due weight.
- **Opinion presented as fact.** "The policy was effective" is an opinion. "According to a 2024 study in *The Lancet*, the policy reduced hospitalizations by 15%" is a sourced factual claim.
- **Fact presented as opinion.** Qualifying well-established scientific consensus with "some believe" or "it is claimed" gives false balance.
- **Promotional tone.** Text that reads like marketing copy — especially common in articles about companies, products, or living persons the student may admire.

### The connection to sourcing

NPOV and sourcing are deeply linked. Biased text often traces back to a sourcing problem: relying on a single perspective, using primary sources for interpretive claims, or selecting only sources that support a preferred conclusion. When the checker flags NPOV concerns, it should also check whether the sourcing is one-sided.

### Why it matters

Students often come from academic writing where they are expected to argue a thesis. Wikipedia is different — it documents and explains, but does not advocate. As WikiEd teaches: "Wikipedia articles should document and explain the major points of view in a balanced and impartial manner."

See: [Wikipedia:Neutral point of view](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Neutral_point_of_view)

## Are these sources Wikipedia trusts? — Reliable sources (WP:RS)

Not all published sources are considered reliable. Wikipedia evaluates sources by their editorial processes, not their conclusions.

### What makes a source reliable

- **Editorial oversight.** Sources with fact-checking, editorial review, and corrections policies are preferred: academic peer-reviewed journals, major newspapers, established reference works, textbooks, and books published by reputable publishers.
- **Reputation for accuracy.** A publication's track record matters. Sources with a history of fabrication or extreme bias are not reliable.
- **Independence from the subject.** Sources should be independent of the subject of the claim (WP:THIRDPARTY). A company's own press release about its founding date is a primary source, not an independent one.
- **Context matters.** A source may be reliable for some claims but not others. A tabloid might reliably report that someone attended an event but not be reliable for medical claims.

### Sources students should avoid

- **Blog posts** and personal websites — no editorial oversight
- **Press materials** intended to promote a particular view
- **Self-published sources** — anyone can publish these; they are acceptable only when published by an established expert whose work has previously appeared in reliable, independent publications
- **Wikipedia itself and its mirrors** — user-generated content cannot validate itself

### Source type hierarchy

- **Secondary sources** (news reporting, scholarship, biographies) are generally strongest — they involve editorial judgment and context.
- **Primary sources** (court records, datasets, official organizational output) are acceptable for straightforward factual claims but should not be used for interpretive or analytical claims.
- **Tertiary sources** (encyclopedias, textbooks, databases) are useful for establishing broad consensus but may be too general for specific claims.

### Accessibility

Sources should not be rejected just because they are behind a paywall, available only in print, or in a non-English language. A citation to a non-English reliable source is valid. When quoting non-English material, a translation should accompany the quote.

Note for checker: LLMs may hallucinate or reconstruct sources they cannot access. Special care must be taken to verify that a source has actually been accessed and read. If you cannot access a source, say so explicitly rather than guessing at its content.

See: [Wikipedia:Reliable sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Reliable_sources)

## Cite at least once per paragraph — When to cite

Not every sentence needs a citation, but Wikipedia has clear expectations about citation density.

### Always cite

- **Direct quotations** — with the source and page number
- **Material challenged or likely to be challenged** — if any reasonable reader might doubt it, cite it
- **Contentious material about living persons** — all contentious BLP claims must be cited
- **Statistics and quantitative claims** — numbers, dates, measurements
- **Claims about specific events** — "On March 3, the company announced..." requires a source
- **Statements of opinion attributed to a source** — "According to Smith, the reform was effective" needs a citation to Smith

### Generally not required

- **Uncontroversial common knowledge** — "Paris is the capital of France" does not need a citation
- **Statements already supported by nearby cited material** — though inline citations are still preferred

### The baseline rule

Wiki Education teaches: **cite at least once per paragraph you contribute.** This is a minimum. Paragraphs with multiple distinct claims should have multiple citations.

See: [Wikipedia:When to cite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:When_to_cite)

## Copy key ideas, not key passages — Close paraphrasing

When you find a good source, it is important to share what you have found without plagiarizing or violating the source's copyright. This is one of the most common problems in student editing.

### What close paraphrasing looks like

Close paraphrasing means changing a few words in a sentence but keeping the original meaning, structure, grammar, and flow of the original text. Even when cited, this violates Wikipedia's copyright policies. It can be easy to unconsciously mimic the structure and word choice of the original source — this is a skill to develop, not a moral failure.

### What the checker looks for

- **Single-source paragraphs** where the text closely follows the structure of the source
- **Quoted-but-not-attributed passages** — text that reads like a quotation but lacks quotation marks
- **Repeated sentence structure** that suggests the text was derived by word-substitution from a source

Note for checker: Detecting close paraphrasing reliably requires comparing article text against source text. The checker can flag suspicious patterns but cannot make a definitive judgment without access to the source. When flagging, explain *why* the pattern is suspicious and suggest the student compare against their source.

### How to paraphrase well

The solution is not to avoid using sources — it is to genuinely understand the material before writing:

1. Read the source carefully.
2. Set it aside and ask yourself: what are the biggest takeaways? What are the results?
3. Write from your understanding, in your own words, using your own sentence structure.
4. Keep a list of key ideas, not key passages of text.
5. If you must use the source's exact words, use quotation marks and cite.

See: [Wikipedia:Close paraphrasing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Close_paraphrasing)

## How do you know this? — No original research (WP:NOR)

Wikipedia reports what reliable sources say. Editors must not synthesize sources to advance a position that none of them individually state.

### What counts as original research

- **Novel synthesis.** Source A says "X happened." Source B says "Y happened." Writing "X caused Y" is original research unless a reliable source explicitly makes that connection.
- **Unpublished facts or data.** Personal observations, experiments, interviews, or surveys conducted by the editor.
- **Novel interpretations.** Applying a published theory to a new situation, even if it seems logical, is original research if no source has published that application.

### What the checker looks for

- **Causal claims not supported by a single source.** If the article says "X led to Y" and cites two different sources (one for X, one for Y), this may be synthesis.
- **Conclusions that go beyond what sources state.** The article may accurately cite sources but draw a connection or implication they do not make.
- **Analytical or interpretive claims supported only by primary sources.** Primary sources support facts; interpretation requires secondary sources.

### The boundary

The line between legitimate editorial judgment (selecting and organizing material from sources) and original research (drawing conclusions beyond what sources state) is one of Wikipedia's most frequent editorial disputes. When in doubt, ask: "Could I point to a single source that says this?"

Note for checker: LLMs should be especially careful here. An LLM's training data gives it broad knowledge that may lead it to treat unsourced connections as obvious. If a connection seems logical but no cited source makes it, flag it as potential synthesis.

See: [Wikipedia:No original research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:No_original_research)

## Strict sourcing for living persons — Biographies of living persons (WP:BLP)

Wikipedia applies its most stringent sourcing standards to articles about living people. This policy extends to all Wikimedia content, including talk pages, user pages, and edit summaries.

### What the checker looks for

- **Any contentious claim about a living person without a citation.** This is the highest-priority flag the checker can raise. Unsourced contentious BLP material must be removed immediately — not tagged, not discussed, but removed.
- **Weak sources for sensitive claims.** Tabloids, self-published sources, and anonymous sources are generally insufficient for contentious claims about living people.
- **Privacy concerns.** Not all verifiable information about a living person belongs in Wikipedia. Sourced material must serve an encyclopedic purpose.

### Why BLP is different

BLP violations are enforced aggressively on Wikipedia. Edit filters automatically flag potential violations. Editors who repeatedly add unsourced contentious material to BLPs may be blocked. For a student editor, a BLP violation is one of the fastest ways to draw negative attention from the community.

See: [Wikipedia:Biographies of living persons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Biographies_of_living_persons)

## Source strength in context — Knowledge equity

Reliable sources are harder to find for some topics than others. Subjects that are underrepresented in mainstream media, academic publishing, or English-language sources may have thinner sourcing landscapes. This is a structural problem, not a reflection of the topic's importance.

### What the checker considers

- **Source type expectations are contextual.** A community newspaper may be the strongest available independent source for a local cultural figure in a region with limited English-language media coverage. This does not make it unreliable — it means the sourcing landscape is different.
- **The distinction between banned and weak.** A personal blog is never an acceptable third-party source, regardless of the topic. But a regional newspaper with editorial oversight, even if not internationally recognized, may be appropriate.
- **Underrepresented topics deserve engagement, not dismissal.** When flagging source quality concerns, the checker should note the topic's sourcing context and distinguish between "this source type is never acceptable on Wikipedia" and "a stronger source would be better if one exists."

### Why this matters

Wiki Education's mission centers on closing knowledge gaps. Students working on underrepresented topics are doing some of Wikipedia's most valuable work. The checker should support that work by applying Wikipedia's policies fairly — neither lowering the bar (Wikipedia's policies apply equally to all topics) nor raising it (by expecting the same depth of sourcing that exists for well-covered topics).

## Is this topic notable? — Notability (WP:N)

Wikipedia requires that a topic have "significant coverage in reliable sources that are independent of the subject" before it warrants its own article. Notability is not about importance — it is about whether enough independent sourcing exists to write a verifiable, neutral article.

### What the checker looks for

- **New articles with few or no independent sources.** If a student is creating a new article, the sources cited should include independent secondary sources that provide significant coverage of the subject — not just passing mentions or primary sources from the subject itself.
- **Sources that are all primary or affiliated.** An article about an organization sourced only from that organization's own website and press releases does not demonstrate notability.
- **Subjects that may not meet general or subject-specific notability guidelines.** Wikipedia has notability guidelines for specific categories (people, organizations, events, academic topics, etc.) with different thresholds.

### Why it matters

Articles that fail notability are nominated for deletion, often quickly. For students, having a carefully written article deleted is demoralizing. The checker should flag notability concerns early so the student can either strengthen the sourcing or choose a different topic.

### The knowledge equity connection

Notability guidelines can be harder to meet for underrepresented topics — fewer reliable sources may have covered the subject. This is a legitimate concern, but the solution is to find the sources that do exist (regional media, specialized publications, government records), not to lower the bar. If independent sources truly do not exist, the topic may not yet be ready for a standalone Wikipedia article.

See: [Wikipedia:Notability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Notability)

## Whose interests are you serving? — Conflict of interest (WP:COI)

Editors with a financial, personal, or organizational relationship to the subject of an article have a conflict of interest. Wikipedia does not ban COI editing outright, but requires disclosure and strongly discourages direct editing of articles where a conflict exists.

### What the checker looks for

- **Students editing articles about their own institution, employer, or organization.** This is common in student assignments where the student chooses a familiar topic.
- **Promotional tone or one-sided sourcing** that suggests advocacy for the subject rather than neutral coverage.
- **Reliance on primary sources from the subject itself** (press releases, official websites, social media) without balancing independent secondary sources.

### What the student should do

- **Disclose the conflict** on the article's talk page using the `{{connected contributor}}` template.
- **Propose changes on the talk page** rather than editing the article directly, especially for contentious or promotional-looking additions.
- **Seek independent sources.** Even if the student knows the subject well, Wikipedia requires that claims be supported by sources independent of the subject.

See: [Wikipedia:Conflict of interest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Conflict_of_interest)

## Check the talk page — Consensus and discussion (WP:CONSENSUS)

Wikipedia decisions are made through discussion and consensus, not by individual editors acting unilaterally. The talk page attached to every article is where editors discuss content disputes, sourcing questions, and major changes.

### What the checker looks for

- **Active disputes on the talk page.** If the talk page has ongoing discussion about the content the student edited — especially sourcing disputes, neutrality concerns, or content removal debates — the checker should flag this so the student is aware.
- **Major changes without discussion.** Significantly rewriting a section, removing substantial sourced content, or adding controversial material to a contentious article without talk page discussion may trigger reverts.
- **Existing maintenance tags or discussions.** The talk page may have `{{citation needed}}` discussions, reliability debates, or WikiProject assessments that are relevant to the student's edit.

### Why this matters for students

New editors' edits receive extra scrutiny. Research shows that ORES, Wikipedia's machine-learning edit quality system, is biased against newcomers — it can overestimate the probability that a new editor's benign edit is damaging. Students who discuss changes on the talk page before or after editing establish good faith and are less likely to have their work summarily reverted.

WikiEd teaches students to be bold, but boldness works best when paired with willingness to discuss. If an edit is reverted, the response is to discuss on the talk page — not to re-add the content (which triggers edit warring, another fast path to sanctions).

See: [Wikipedia:Consensus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Consensus)
